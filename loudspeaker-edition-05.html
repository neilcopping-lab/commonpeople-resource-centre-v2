A loudspeaker on the desk, a city in the rain, and a month that rewards a second look.

00

From the editor.

Here is a thing I keep bumping into. We spend our days managing the workplace we imagine, not the one that is actually sat in front of us. We decide someone is skiving because they nipped out to the shop, and they turn round and tell us it is the very thing that keeps them productive. We tell ourselves people will come to us when something is wrong, and half of them worked out long ago that we would really rather they didn't.

Once you spot it, you see it everywhere this month. Kids turn up with skills they cannot put a name to, so we call them not ready. We build proper pay scales and then hide them like state secrets. We write off a thirty-something apprentice on a spreadsheet and a disabled colleague on a missing bit of paper. The jobs market even started turning while most of us were still bracing for a worse winter. Nearly every one of these has the same fix, and it is dead simple: look again at what is really going on.

There is a Budget on 28 October, which is the time of year everyone assumes the worst and every now and then turns out to be right. We will see. Until then, the cheapest tool in the box is a second, honest look. That is the whole paper this month.

The brief has not changed. Useful, current, properly British and never dull.

“To see what is in front of one's nose needs a constant struggle.”

George Orwell, in his 1946 essay In Front of Your Nose
01

The lead.

Lead · HR & public policy · The Times · 19 October 2026

The Prime Minister calls the NEET numbers a moral failure. The fix starts with 62,400 openings.

Almost one million 16 to 24 year olds in the UK are not in education, employment or training (NEET), the figure the ONS last put at 981,000 for April to June 2026. Writing in The Times on 19 October, Prime Minister Andy Burnham called it an “economic catastrophe and a moral failure”, and framed it as a choice: “We either invest in the success of our young people or pay for failure through the benefits system. We cannot afford to do both. I choose the first.” The welfare system, he argued, currently offers “an assessment, a payment and, after that, a goodbye.”

Behind the rhetoric sit some actual commitments. The Government has begun rolling out a series of sector “jobs plans”, starting with two. Professional services firms, in accountancy, law and consultancy, have agreed to create 22,400 apprenticeships a year for under-25s. Construction firms have agreed a further 40,000 placements. That is 62,400 openings between them, with digital, technology and defence plans promised next. Business Secretary Jonathan Reynolds pitched it as “giving workers the skills they need to get good jobs in growing industries”, while Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden talked of building “clear pathways” into apprenticeships and work. Separately, the Chancellor, John Healey, has put £100m into expanding local apprenticeship brokerage, teams he likened to football scouts, across England's mayoral authorities from 2027.

The harder argument is about funding. Alan Milburn, the former health secretary now leading the Government's Young People and Work review, has pointed out that for every £25 spent on benefits for young people out of work, just £1 goes on helping them find a job, and that only one in five get any meaningful employment support. His interim report diagnosed a fragmented system of schools, colleges, councils and the NHS all pulling separately; the part with the actual solutions is due after the Budget. Writing in the Guardian on 18 October, he argued for shifting money from welfare to work support, and was blunt about the caricature of a workshy, snowflake generation: the overwhelming majority of young people who are NEET, he pointed out, want to work.

Not everyone is sold. The Conservatives have criticised the plans, with Shadow Business Secretary Julia Lopez arguing that the Government's own tax rises and red tape are destroying job opportunities for young people. The party has its own offer, set out by shadow chancellor Andrew Griffith in early October: a pledge to halve employer National Insurance for 21 to 24 year olds, from 15% to 7.5%, which it costs at around £2.3bn. Labour dismissed that as “fantasy economics”.

For employers, the direction of travel is hard to miss. Government money and political attention are tilting towards getting under-25s into work, through apprenticeships, placements and hiring incentives, and away from simply paying them to stay out of it. Whether 62,400 openings and a Budget-day rethink of the welfare pound can move a near-million number is the question everyone will be watching.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Map your early-careers pipeline against where the money and incentives are about to flow: professional services apprenticeships, construction placements and under-25 hiring support. For once, if you employ young people, the subsidies and the political wind are at your back.

Neil's take

Here is my problem with all this, and it does make me angry. It is welcome, but you are cutting the weeds and leaving the roots. The real failure is not this Government's alone, it is twenty years of party after party with no strategy for how a generation grows up ready for work. And look at who writes these plans. We ask the policymakers, we ask the think tanks, we clap the bloke in the designer suit with a white tee under it instead of a shirt and tie, and not once do we ask the 16 to 24 year olds what they would actually do. When did we last sit a young person down and ask them how they would fix it? When someone finally does ask, in a Survation survey for the BBC this month, more than four in ten said they want more real work experience and more help applying for a job, and over half had never had any. They are not workshy, they are shut out. And not just the ones off to university. The ones stuck in that group right now, and the thirteen year olds sat in school today already losing hope before they have even started. And start at the root, in schools: how many of them have special educational needs, a disability, or are struggling with their mental health, bounced between health, education and employment services that never talk to each other? It has to be joined up. 62,400 openings will not dent a million, and they are aimed at a handful of sectors. Not everyone is an engineer. Where are the carers, the nurses, the teachers, the scientists, the data analysts, the tree surgeons, the designers, the chefs, the ecologists, the sound engineers, the stonemasons, even the beekeepers and the farriers? And look at the £100m they have just thrown at “football scouts” to go and find apprenticeships, when every college in the country already has people doing exactly that, me included. Back the people already on the ground, do not build a new layer on top of them. It is government policy that just does not join up with education, or with what is already happening on the ground. And here is where the real money comes from. Not just the unspent levy. The businesses pulling in millions, even billions, in profit every year should be asked, or made, to put one per cent of it back into fixing this, giving back to the very people who helped build those profits in the first place. And before anyone tells me it cannot be done, the sums are HMRC's own: 7,670 companies pay 60% of all our Corporation Tax, and one per cent of the profit at the top would raise somewhere between £2bn and £4.5bn a year. That is three times the wasted levy, and enough to cover the full first-year wage of well over a hundred thousand young apprentices. The money is there. It always was. That is not a left idea or a right idea, it is just a decent one, which is exactly why the politics has to come out of it. Get every party in the room, and put young people, educators and the charities already doing this in there with them. Then change the one thing that actually moves the needle: whether an employer will give a young person a chance. The best question I have heard all year is still the simplest. Who gave you your first chance? Now it's your turn.

The theme of the month: look again at what is actually there.

The stories.

01 · HR News & Trends · LiveCareer, via Allwork.Space · 13 October 2026

Three in four of us run errands on work time, and swear it makes us better at the job.

LiveCareer went and asked 1,500 workers what the day actually looks like, and it turns out we are a nation of multitaskers. 76% of us deal with a bit of personal life during work hours at least a few times a week, and 48% do it every single day. Before you clutch your pearls, the top three are gloriously boring: the washing (54%), nipping out for an errand (51%) and a scroll through the socials (50%). Most of us keep it short, an hour or less.

Here is the bit I like. 86% reckon a quick breather actually makes them better at the job, and only 3% think it does any harm. And we are a good deal more relaxed about it than the rest of Europe, where only 27% own up to doing it daily. Call it skiving if it makes you feel better. The people doing it call it getting through the day.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Before you draft a policy banning the mid-morning shop, ask yourself what you are actually measuring: the work, or the bums on seats. If the work lands, a ten-minute errand was never your problem.

02 · Apprenticeships · FE Week · 17 October 2026

The plan to get more young apprentices? Charge employers more for the older ones.

Word is the Government is looking at making employers stump up a quarter of the training bill for apprentices aged 25 and over, where the smaller firms currently pay nothing. FE Week says it is not confirmed, would kick in from September 2027, and would drop the funding from 95% to 75%. Under-25s keep the lot.

One government insider called the thinking “nonsensical”, and they have a point. Your over-25 apprentice is the career-changer, the parent coming back, the backbone of care and engineering. Make them cost more and you do not get younger apprentices, you just get fewer apprentices. While everyone is cheering the youth jobs plans (see the lead), this is the quiet one that lands straight on your training budget.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

If you train over-25s, get that 25% into your 2027/28 budget now, and work out which standards you would still pay for if the rumour turns real.

03 · Getting your people employment ready · Skills England · 5 October 2026

Young people have the skills. Now there is a free crib sheet for naming them.

Skills England has put out a free Employability Skills Framework, and it is quietly brilliant for a simple reason: it fixes a daft mismatch. Young people turn up loaded with skills they cannot describe, and we cannot see past the thin CV. It names 13 everyday skills, from planning and organising to listening, problem solving and digital know-how, and spells out what each one looks like at the start of a career and how you grow it.

It even translates every one of them for an AI-shaped workplace, which is where a lot of first jobs now live. It was built with The King's Trust, the Youth Futures Foundation and others, and I love the plainness of the framing. In their own words, opportunity should not depend on whether you have already had a job. If you run work experience or hire your first youngsters, it is a ready-made common language.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Hand the 13-skill list to your hiring managers and get them scoring young candidates on evidence, not polish. It turns “not quite ready” into something you can actually do something about.

04 · Recruitment · KPMG and REC · 10 October 2026

The jobs market is flickering back to life, for the first time in four years.

After four years of everyone staring at the floor, the jobs market has lifted its head. The October KPMG and REC Report on Jobs has permanent placements up for a second month on the bounce, the fastest in four years. Demand is still slipping, but the gentlest it has in over two years, and private-sector permanent vacancies grew for the first time in more than two years. That flood of applicants is finally easing too.

Nobody is cracking open the good biscuits yet. KPMG's Jon Holt called it “a fragile recovery”, and the REC's Maxine Bligh said the market is “revving its engine” while nudging the Chancellor not to stall it. Pay growth for new hires actually cooled. Put it together and it is the first proper green shoot in a long old winter, with a Budget on 28 October that could water it or tread on it.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

If you have been sat on a vacancy waiting for the bottom, the numbers say it may have been and gone. Get your pipeline lined up before the competition reads the same report.

Read it on KPMG →

05 · Employee value proposition · Paydata · 8 October 2026

You built the pay scales. You are just not telling anyone what is on them.

Paydata's Autumn 2026 reward survey has the median pay rise at 3.0%, two-thirds of firms budgeting 3% or less, and money worries front of mind: 55% say affordability weighs heavily on what they pay. But the stat that made me laugh out loud is the transparency one. Three-quarters of employers have built proper pay scales, 64% have formal job grading, and only 36% will tell a soul what the ranges are.

That is a lot of lovely machinery left in a locked drawer. We do the hard graft of building fair, structured pay, then keep it a secret, while candidates increasingly bin any advert that will not show the money. Nearly half of firms also admit paying newcomers enough to open awkward gaps with the people already there. Transparency is not a nice-to-have. It is the difference between a structure that reassures your team and a secret that quietly unsettles them.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

You have probably already built the ranges. Pick three roles and put the bands in the next adverts, but tell your existing people first, so nobody finds out what they are worth from a job post.

06 · Wellbeing · Health Shield, via HRreview · 5 October 2026

You think your people will open up. They think you would rather they didn't.

Health Shield's 2026 Britain at Work report, with Censuswide polling 2,002 employees and 500 HR folk, caught us out nicely. On mental health, 74% of employers reckon their people would happily bring it up. Ask the people, and it is 56%. On physical health it is 84% against 67%. And the daddy of them all, money: 68% of bosses think staff would talk about money worries, and just 46% actually would.

The two groups were asked separately, so this is about perception, not proof that any one manager has misread their own lot. But it is a useful cold flannel to the face. As Health Shield's Paul Shires puts it, do not assume people will always feel able to raise the hard stuff to your face. An open-door policy only counts if your people actually believe the door is open.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Stop judging your wellbeing offer by how many people come to you. Put an anonymous route in and watch whether the quiet ones start talking. The silence probably is not everyone being fine.

Read it on HRreview →

07 · Working parents · Acas · 1 October 2026

Flexible working is about to grow teeth, and parents will feel it first.

Flexible working is about to get some teeth, and if you employ parents you will feel it first. Acas has opened a consultation on a new Code of Practice for handling flexible-working requests, off the back of the Government's response in September. Under the Employment Rights Act, a flat “no” will not cut it anymore. You will have to actually meet, talk it through, and then show why saying no was reasonable.

The draft lays out four principles: decide on the evidence, try to make the request work, find a solution together, and be consistent. Acas reckons nearly 8 in 10 of us say flexible working matters when we pick a job. It lands around autumn 2027 and the consultation runs to 17 December, so you have got time to sort your process rather than panic later. Their own line sums it up: you can only say no if it is reasonable to.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Dig out your last ten flexible-working refusals and ask, honestly, could you show each was reasonable? If the real answer is “because we always say no”, you have got work to do before 2027.

Read it on Acas →

08 · Employing people with seen and unseen disabilities · DWP · 2 October 2026

The Government's neurodiversity verdict: design the job for the person, not the paperwork.

On 2 October the DWP answered the Expert Academic Panel on Neurodiversity, and the gist is refreshingly sensible: “needs-led” support. Help the person with the actual thing they find hard in the actual job, instead of sitting on your hands waiting for a diagnosis. It backs a reboot of Disability Confident for more honesty and teeth, is running a 16-week look at universal workplace design, and is weighing a two-week deadline for answering adjustment requests.

There is a bit of substance behind the warm words too: more than 1,800 employer reps trained on neurodiversity this year, and Access to Work staffing set to rise by over 70%. The line to keep is the panel's own, and the department repeated it: a diagnosis should not mean being signed off, written off, or left with no hope of a job. Most neuro-inclusive fixes cost next to nothing. What is usually in the way is habit, not money.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Pick one role this quarter and design it for a human: clear instructions, a quiet corner, flexible hours, things in writing. Sort it for a neurodivergent employee and you have almost always made it better for everyone.

Our campaign.

Running this month from The Com'mon People

Pay the first year.

Here is the maths that keeps me up at night. Big employers will pay around £4.5bn in apprenticeship levy this year, and FE Week reckons about £700m of it will never reach an apprenticeship. Meanwhile nearly a million young people are sat out of work and study. So our #PayTheFirstYear campaign asks the Government to take that unspent levy and use it to pay a young apprentice's first-year wage at small and medium firms, sized to headcount, new jobs only. It runs for four weeks from 19 October, right around the Budget on 28 October.

The ask is dead simple: let unspent levy pay the first-year wage, at no less than the National Minimum Wage for the apprentice's age, for 16 to 24 year olds at firms with fewer than 250 staff. The training is already paid for. The wage is the gap. That is it.

“Pay the training. Pay the first year. Then watch who sticks around.”
