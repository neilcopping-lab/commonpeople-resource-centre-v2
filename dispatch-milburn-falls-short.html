The Dispatch · The Milburn IssueNovember 2026
Milburn’s hiring grant: £3,000. A first-year apprentice wage: £15,600.

Where Milburn
falls short.

Alan Milburn’s final report gets the diagnosis right. But for the small businesses that used to give most school leavers their first break, it offers a modest grant when the real barrier is the wage. The money to fix that has already been collected. Here is the case, every number sourced, and a petition you can sign.

In a nutshell

  • Alan Milburn's final report gets the diagnosis right: 1 in 8 young people are not in education, employment or training.
  • But it offers small firms a £3,000 grant against a £15,600 first-year apprentice wage.
  • The fix it missed is to pay the first year, using levy money that never reaches apprentices. There's a petition to sign.

Alan Milburn’s final report on young people and work landed this week, and I want to start by giving him his due. He has looked a hard problem in the eye and refused to blame young people for it. He calls for a complete reset, sets a big target, and puts apprenticeships at the centre.

But I spent nine years in recruitment and now work with employers on apprenticeships every day, and I can tell you where the plan runs out of road. It leans on new public money. It moves slowly. And for the small businesses that used to give most school leavers their first break, it offers a modest grant when the real barrier is the wage.

The good news is that the money to fix that has already been collected.

01 · What Milburn got right

The diagnosis is
hard to argue with.

1 in 8young people are not in education, employment or training. Six in ten of them have never had a job.
Source [1]
1.27myoung people could be NEET by 2031 on current policy.
Source [1]
−40%youth apprenticeships over the past decade, in the government’s own words.
Source [4]

Among European countries in 2025, only Romania had a higher youth NEET rate than the UK.[1] Apprenticeship starts for 16 to 24 year olds have fallen by 35% since the levy arrived in 2017.[2] Milburn puts it bluntly: Britain has had “a youth NEET problem for 25 years and never a plan to end it”.[1]

His plan sets out to cut the NEET rate from 13% to around 5% by 2036, with a local Youth Opportunity Service in every part of England by 2029 and new legal guarantees behind it. He wants apprenticeships doubled over the decade, and a £3,000 grant for employers who hire a young person who has been NEET for six months.[1]

02 · Where it falls short

Five gaps.
One of them is the wage.

1. The incentive is too small for small businesses.

Milburn’s £3,000 grant covers less than a fifth of a first-year wage. For a café, a salon or a family engineering firm, it doesn’t change the maths. Training for under-25s at small firms is already free from August 2026.[10] The wage is the cost nobody is helping with.

2. It waits for young people to fall through the net.

The grant only applies once someone has been NEET for six months. I would rather catch them on the way out of school or college, before the damage is done.

3. It relies on new money and takes time.

Critics on both sides of the Commons have said so. The Conservatives argue nothing will happen for at least three years, and the Liberal Democrats warn that no target can create jobs that don’t exist.[1]

4. The unspent levy is left on the table.

Milburn himself has said that the apprenticeship system and the levy have failed.[3] Yet the coverage of his final report says nothing about the hundreds of millions of levy that never reach an apprentice.

5. It says nothing about where the money is spent.

More apprenticeships matter. So does where the training happens, and who benefits from the money.

03 · The money is already there

£780 million
that never landed.

Every large employer pays a skills tax that is meant to train the next generation. A big slice of it never gets there. Last year, 2025-26, FE Week estimated that around £780 million of levy receipts was kept by the Treasury rather than spent on apprenticeships.[6] This year, employers are forecast to pay £4.5 billion, leaving an estimated £700 million not returned to the system.[7]

It isn’t a one-off. Between 2017-18 and 2023-24, almost £2.2 billion of England’s apprenticeship budget was handed back to the Treasury.[8] And the clock is speeding up: new levy funds now expire after 12 months instead of 24.[9]

Meanwhile small firms have stepped back. Since the levy arrived, apprenticeship starts at small and medium businesses have fallen by 48%.[5]

04 · Our answer

Pay the
first year.

The apprenticeships are there. The colleges are there. The young people are there. What is missing is employers, especially small ones, able to take the risk on the wage. So I am asking the Government to pay the full first-year wage, at the statutory apprentice rate, for 16 to 24 year olds taken on by small and medium sized businesses, funded from unspent levy and topped up by a 1% of profit contribution from the most profitable companies.Opinion · the proposal, stated plainly.
How it keeps the job at the end

Only the firms that need it. Employers with fewer than 250 staff that don’t pay the levy. Large levy payers already have their own pot.

Keep them, get the rest. The wage is paid monthly, with the last three months released once the apprentice reaches 12 months in the job, so the money rewards keeping them on.

New jobs only. Payments pause if a firm’s other headcount falls, so nobody is replaced.

Start here. A pilot in Hertfordshire, already one of the Jobs Guarantee areas.[12]

This isn’t as radical as it sounds. Kickstart paid for 25 hours a week of work for six months, and for every 100 young people who took part, 11 more were in unsubsidised work two years later than a similar group who didn’t.[13] The Jobs Guarantee covers 100% of employment costs for 25 hours a week today.[12] The Fabian Society has proposed letting levy funds cover half of foundation apprentices’ wages.[14] I would go further than half.

Small businesses get a year to prove the value of a young person with no wage risk. Young people get a real job, not a scheme. And unspent levy finally does what it was meant to do.

05 · Keep it local

Spend it where
your people live.

Where the levy is spent matters too. Independent training providers delivered 65% of apprenticeship starts in the first half of this year.[15] As of May 2023, eight of the ten largest providers were majority-owned by private equity firms or other global investors, and only four of those eight were headquartered in the UK.[16] Many do excellent work, but their profits belong to their investors, wherever those investors are.

£11.60of economic benefit for every £1 of public funding invested in Waltham Forest College.
London Economics · Source [17]
86%of 16 to 24 year olds said they wanted a job close to where they live.
BAE Systems research · Source [18]

So to every large employer: spend your levy where your business and your people live. You can transfer up to 50% of the levy entering your account each year to other employers,[19] so fund apprentices in small businesses in your own town before the 12-month clock runs out. The full case is in Keep your levy or apprenticeship funding in your town.[22]

06 · Sign the petition

Every signature
adds up.

Official UK Government and Parliament petition

Use unspent apprenticeship levy to pay young apprentices’ first-year wages.

At 10,000 signatures the Government must respond. At 100,000 it is considered for a debate in Parliament.[20] Any British citizen or UK resident can sign, once. It takes a minute.

Sign the petition →

The petition needs supporters before Parliament checks and publishes it. Add your name now and help get it there.

If you run a business, there is one more thing you can do: commit to spending your levy where your people live, and tell us you’re in with #KeepItLocal. If you’re a councillor or an MP, back a Hertfordshire pilot and ask one simple question in public: where did last year’s unspent levy go?

Young people are not the skills gap. They are the answer to it. Let’s give small firms the means to take the first chance on them.
