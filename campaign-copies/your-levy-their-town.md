I spent nine years in recruitment before I joined Oaklands College. In that time I worked with a lot of leaders who spoke, with real pride, about "our community". Now, in my role at Oaklands, I find myself asking those same kinds of leaders a different question. Where does your apprenticeship levy actually go?

So here is the question I want every chief executive and every Head of People in the country to sit with for a minute. Your business pays the levy. Your head office, your sites, your customers and your next generation of staff all live in real towns. So why does so much of that money end up somewhere else?

This is my case for keeping it local. Where it is my opinion, I say so. Where it is a fact, there is a source number next to it so you can check my working.

01 · First, follow the money

## First, follow the money

Any employer with a paybill over £3 million pays the apprenticeship levy at 0.5%.[1] The Office for Budget Responsibility expects it to raise £4.4 billion in 2025-26.[2] Not all of that comes back as training. England spent £3.095 billion on apprenticeships in 2025-26,[3] and FE Week has estimated that, after around £500 million goes to the devolved nations, the Treasury is effectively keeping around £780 million of the gap between levy receipts and the apprenticeship budget.[2] Between 2017-18 and 2023-24, almost £2.2 billion of England's apprenticeship budget was handed back to the Treasury.[4]

And the rules have just got tighter. From the 2026/27 academic year the government is removing the 10% top-up on levy accounts, cutting the time you have to spend your funds from 24 months to 12, and asking levy payers to pay 25% of training costs once their pot is empty, up from 5%.[5] In plain English: levy you do not plan for will now disappear twice as fast.

### Where the money that is spent ends up

Independent training providers delivered 65% of apprenticeship starts between August 2025 and January 2026. Colleges delivered 19%.[6] An FE Week analysis found that, as of May 2023, eight of the ten largest providers by apprenticeship starts were majority-owned by private equity firms or other global investors, and only four of those eight were headquartered in the UK.[7]

Let me be clear, because this matters to me. This is not an attack on independent providers. Many do excellent work, and the same FE Week analysis found seven of the largest private equity-backed providers were rated good or better by Ofsted.[7] My point is simpler. When a provider is owned by investors, any profit it makes belongs to those investors, wherever they are. In my view, that is a choice your business is making, whether it means to or not.

02 · Why local makes a bigger difference

## Why local makes a bigger difference

### Who owns a college?

Further education corporations in England, the legal bodies set up to run colleges like Oaklands, are exempt charities.[8] Since November 2022 the Office for National Statistics has classified them as part of central government for the national accounts.[9]

Take Oaklands as an example. Oaklands College is run by Oaklands Further Education Corporation, which was set up under the Further and Higher Education Act 1992. The college is an exempt charity, and the members of the Corporation are the trustees of that charity.[28] The Corporation is made up of external, staff and student governors, who are collectively responsible for the college's strategic direction and for making sure its resources are used well.[29]

Oaklands' published accounts for 2023/24 set a financial objective of generating cash for reinvestment, to cover capital spending funded from the college's own reserves.[28] We have three campuses, in St Albans, Welwyn Garden City and Borehamwood, plus an arable farm, across an estate of 462 acres. In 2023-24 we reached more than 18,000 school-age children through assemblies, careers fairs and guidance sessions.[10] I would argue many of those children are the future workforce of the businesses around us.

### Money that stays, works harder

Economists call this the local multiplier. The New Economics Foundation built a tool, LM3, to measure how money that enters a local economy gets spent and then re-spent there, and how money spent with organisations outside the area simply leaks away.[11] Colleges can show this in hard numbers. Independent analysis by London Economics found that every £1 of public funding invested in Waltham Forest College generated £11.60 of economic benefit, with a total economic impact of £304 million in 2022-23.[12]

### Your future talent lives on your doorstep

In research for BAE Systems, 86% of 16 to 24 year olds surveyed said they wanted a job close to where they live.[13] In April to June 2026, 981,000 young people in the UK aged 16 to 24 were not in education, employment or training. That is 13.0% of the age group.[14]

Yet the levy is drifting away from them. In 2024-25, apprenticeship starts by under-19s fell 5% to 74,990, while level 7 starts rose 41% to 33,560.[15] Under-19s made up just 21.2% of all starts.[16] At the same time, 27% of all vacancies in 2024 were hard to fill because applicants lacked the skills, qualifications or experience needed.[17]

I am not knocking leadership training. Good managers matter, and I have said many times that apprentices often leave because of how they are managed. But in my view the balance is wrong, and too few young people near your sites are getting a way in.

**Your levy can build a provider's balance sheet somewhere else, or it can build the town your staff go home to. Both are legal. Only one of them is a strategy.** Neil Copping

03 · So why don't large employers do this?

## So why don't large employers do this?

This is the question I really want answered, so here is my honest view from both sides of the desk.

- **One contract is easier than twenty.** Large employers want consistent quality, a named account manager, and fast, accurate data. BT's Head of Apprenticeships set out exactly that list when describing what a large employer needs from a provider.[18] A national provider can offer one contract, one portal and one invoice. A patchwork of colleges, historically, has not.

- **Whoever knocks first usually wins.** A 2013 government evaluation found the most common trigger for an employer first getting involved in apprenticeships was being approached by a training provider, at 37%.[19] In my experience, national providers have large sales teams. Most colleges have a handful of people like me.

- **The levy sits at head office, but the need sits on site.** In my view, levy decisions are usually made by central L&D or procurement teams, a long way from the stores, depots and kitchens where entry-level roles are hardest to fill.

- **The spend has moved up the levels.** With level 7 starts up 41% in a year,[15] the money has followed senior programmes. Looking at apprenticeship achievements at undergraduate level and above, the three largest providers in a recent quarter were national names: Kaplan, Corndel and BPP.[20]

- **Old perceptions.** In my experience, many leaders still think of colleges as places for 16 year olds. In fact, the Institute for Fiscal Studies reports that colleges in England educate around 1.6 million learners, including 925,000 adults.[21]

- **Colleges haven't always made it easy.** I will own this one. In my view, too often we have waited for employers to come to us, talked in jargon and been slow to respond. That has to change.

04 · How colleges and universities break down the barriers

## How colleges and universities break down the barriers

If we want this money, we have to earn it. Here is what I believe colleges and universities need to do, and some proof that it already works.

- **Club together and offer one front door.** It has been done. A group of colleges has run national apprenticeship partnerships with employers including BAM, Mitie, Sodexo and Kier.[22] BAM said the partnership gave it "a consistent approach for delivering apprenticeships at a regional level" across its national business.[22] One contract, local delivery. That is the model.

- **Run it like a national account.** A named account manager, agreed service levels, regular data reports and one point of contact. Large employers expect it, so we should offer it before we are asked.[18] At Oaklands, a strategic partnership agreement is already one of the options in our employer partnership programme.[31]

- **Make it a two-way partnership, not just a contract.** From April 2026 the growth and skills levy can also fund some non-apprenticeship training.[27] At Oaklands we bring it all together in the Industry Advantage Scheme, our free employer partnership programme, built as a structured partnership with tangible benefits on both sides.[31] Employers choose at least three ways to support students, across work placements and projects, student engagement and mentoring, curriculum shaping, and recruitment and strategic support. In return they get staff upskilling and training, a talent pipeline, networking and brand visibility, and campus perks, starting with a free skills audit that covers funded English, maths and digital skills training.[31][32]

- **Prove the local impact in numbers.** The Procurement Act 2023 came into force on 24 February 2025, and the National Procurement Policy Statement encourages suppliers to recruit young people and remove barriers for under-represented groups.[23] The government has also consulted on requiring a jobs and skills award criterion for public contracts over £5 million.[24] If your business bids for public work, local apprenticeships are evidence you can put in front of a buyer. Colleges should hand you that evidence, ready to use.

- **Use the transfer.** Levy payers can transfer up to 50% of their levy funds each year to other businesses of their choice, including their own supply chain.[25] National brands are already backing colleges directly: Topps Tiles launched a national tiling apprenticeship partnership with Leeds College of Building and South & City College Birmingham in 2025.[26]

- **Ask for all of it.** You don't need one provider for head office and another for the shop floor. Colleges already join forces to deliver national programmes for national employers, and BAM, Mitie, Sodexo and Kier have all done it.[22] So bring the lot: your leadership and national programmes, delivered by colleges working together, right alongside your entry-level and technical apprenticeships. One national partnership, delivered locally, in the towns where your people live and work.

05 · Set it at the top, own it on the ground

## Set it at the top, own it on the ground

Reason three above is the one I think matters most. The levy sits at head office, but the need sits on site. So here is my suggestion for fixing it, and it costs nothing.

Write the policy centrally. Decide how much of your levy you want spent locally, what good looks like and how you will report it. Then hand it to the people who run your regions and operations. Your regional managers, operations managers and ops directors know which sites are short of chefs, technicians or team leaders. Give them the job of supporting their unit managers to make it happen, and let each unit manager own the relationship with their local college.

#### Set it at the top, own it on the ground

- 1**Head office** Sets the policy: the local share, what good looks like and how it is reported.

- 2**Regional and operations leaders** Support their unit managers to make it happen.

- 3**Unit managers** Own the relationship with their local college.

Opinion: the author’s suggested model.

Why does that matter? In my experience, people look after what they own, and the research points the same way. A University of Birmingham study, using two years of data from 20,000 employees, found that people with more autonomy at work reported higher wellbeing and job satisfaction.[33] The CIPD adds an important catch: autonomy should be backed up by formal policies and well-defined boundaries.[34] That is exactly the model. Head office sets the frame. The people on the ground fill it in.

It is not as radical as it sounds, either. In the government's 2015 employer survey, more than half (56%) of branch sites made their apprenticeship recruitment decisions without any head office input.[35] The decisions are often made locally anyway. The levy just has not followed them.

### What else your business gets back

- **Skills that fit the job.** In the government's latest employer evaluation, 86% of employers said apprenticeships developed skills relevant to their organisation, and 77% reported increased productivity.[36]

- **People who stay.** Among employers who trained existing staff as apprentices, 75% reported improved staff retention, and almost seven in ten (69%) had kept on at least one apprentice after they finished.[36] In my view, someone who lives ten minutes from the site is easier to keep than someone with an hour's commute.

- **Managers who grow.** In my view, a unit manager who runs their own college relationship gets better at spotting, hiring and developing people. That is a leadership skill your business keeps.

- **A story worth telling.** Local apprenticeships give you social value evidence for public tenders, real stories for your ESG report and genuine goodwill in the towns where your customers live.

### “Our local college doesn’t do that apprenticeship”

I hear this a lot, and it is rarely the end of the conversation. There are 212 colleges in England.[37] If the college nearest your site does not run the apprenticeship you need, in my experience there is usually another college in the same region that does. The government’s Find apprenticeship training service lets you enter the town or postcode of your workplace and see which training providers deliver that course in your location.[38] If nobody does, you can share your interest with all training providers.[39]

And it is worth building those relationships directly. Every area of England has a local skills improvement plan, led by an employer representative body such as a chamber of commerce, which sets out the skills local employers need so colleges and training providers can line up their courses.[40][41][42] When your managers talk to colleges themselves, you build a talent pipeline on your doorstep, you get a say in filling your local skills gaps, and you strengthen your corporate social responsibility with something you can actually point to. Three wins from one phone call.

06 · The call to arms

## The call to arms

The best partnerships work both ways. So here is my ask: five things any national employer could do this quarter.

- **Map it.** Put your workforce postcodes next to your levy spend. Where do your people live, and where does your money go?

- **Set a local share, then hand it over.** Decide what percentage of your levy should go to providers based where your sites and staff are, and track it like any other target. Then give your regional and operations leaders the job of making it happen, with each unit manager owning their college relationship.

- **Pledge your transfer locally.** Use up to 50% of your levy[25] to fund apprentices in small businesses in your own supply chain and community, before the 12-month clock runs out.[5]

- **Join your local college's employer partnership.** At Oaklands that is the free Industry Advantage Scheme. Choose at least three ways to support students, such as a work experience placement (minimum 30 hours), a T Level industry placement (315 hours, about 45 days), industry board attendance or apprenticeship recruitment.[31] And next time your apprenticeship contract is up for renewal, put a college or college group on the tender list. If your nearest college doesn’t run the apprenticeship you need, ask it to point you to one that does, or search Find apprenticeship training.[38]

- **Report it.** Add local apprenticeship spend to your social value and ESG reporting, so your board can see it.

And to my colleagues in colleges and universities: let's make it easy for them to say yes.

I know an article like this won't change every mind. But as I always tell my family, you don't know if you don't try. If you lead a business with people in Hertfordshire, I would love to talk about how the Industry Advantage Scheme could work for you. If you don't, call the college nearest your head office and start the conversation.
