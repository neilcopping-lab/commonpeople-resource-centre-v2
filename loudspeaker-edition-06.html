This is the month the Employment Rights Act stopped being a slide deck and started doing the talking. Properly British. Never dull.

Published on LinkedIn on the last Friday of the month, with a full web edition here.

Share this

The law found its voice this month. Time to listen.

00

From the editor.

For eighteen months the Employment Rights Act was a thing we talked about in the future tense. A webinar. A slide that said "watch this space". Something for the lawyers to worry about later. This month it grew legs, walked into the building and started rearranging the furniture.

You can see the shape of it in the stories below. A union walked into Google's UK offices because the law now says it can. Bereaved parents gained a day-one right that did not exist a year ago. A review nobody had heard of in the spring is suddenly sitting in three quarters of employers' wellbeing plans. The thread this month is simple: the rules changed while we were arguing about whether they would, and the quietly competent employers are the ones who read the small print first.

The rest, as ever, is Britain being Britain. We are building submarines and cannot find enough welders, drowning in job applications that a machine wrote, and marking a pay-gap day most people have never heard of. Make a brew. It is a good one.

The brief has not changed. Useful, current, properly British and never dull.

"I will fight for what I believe in until I drop dead. And that's what keeps you alive."

Barbara Castle, Labour cabinet minister and architect of the 1970 Equal Pay Act

A door held open: the law now lets the union in.

01

The stories.

01 · HR News & Trends · ITPro / HRreview · November 2026

A union just walked into Google's UK offices, and the law held the door.

The trade union Prospect has secured a workplace access agreement with Google that reaches around 7,000 of its UK staff, one of the first big-tech deals struck under the new right of access created by the Employment Rights Act. In plain terms, union organisers can now talk to workers on company premises and through company channels, something that was entirely at the employer's gift until this autumn. For a sector that has spent two decades treating unions as a rumour, that is a genuine shift.

Note what it is not. Google has stopped short of full statutory recognition, offering conciliation talks instead, and two other unions have grumbled that Prospect got a soft deal. Recognition is the bigger prize and this is not it. But access is the doorway recognition walks through, and the point for the rest of us is that the mechanism now exists whether you fancy it or not. If a union wants in, "no" is no longer a complete sentence.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Find out, before someone else does, whether you would even know how to respond to an access request. Draft a one-page position now, while it is hypothetical and calm, rather than later, when it is neither.

02 · Apprenticeships · GOV.UK / UK Defence Journal · November 2026

We are building nuclear submarines, and we cannot find the welders.

Britain's defence nuclear programme is going on a hiring spree, with the sector aiming for around 22,000 apprenticeships as part of a skills drive worth roughly 290 million pounds. The numbers behind it are eye-watering: the Dreadnought and SSN-AUKUS submarine programmes, plus the warhead work, are expected to push the defence nuclear workforce to about 65,000 people by 2030. That is a lot of reactors, and a lot of very specific trades, to staff from a standing start.

It is also a useful mirror. If the state can decide that apprenticeships are the engine of a 30-year industrial bet, the "apprentices are for other, bigger firms" line gets harder to hold with a straight face. The hard part was never the funding headline. It is the patience: nuclear-grade welders and technicians take years to grow, which means the only sensible time to start is roughly a decade before you need them.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Pick the one role you always struggle to recruit and ask whether you could grow it instead of buying it. A two-year apprenticeship started now beats another three years of failed searches for the finished article.

03 · Getting your people employment ready · GOV.UK · 15 November 2026

The state will pay for a young person's first six months. You just have to host them.

The government's jobs guarantee is going national, with around 90,000 young people furthest from work to be offered a guaranteed, paid placement "no matter where they live". It sits inside a wider package the government says will reach close to a million young people through training and work experience. The scheme funds a six-month job for 18 to 24 year olds who have been stuck outside work, education or training, and it needs one thing employers have not always offered: somewhere to actually do the work.

This is where "employment ready" quietly flips. For years the debate has been about fixing the young person before they arrive. A funded placement turns it around: the readiness is built on the job, in your building, at low cost and low risk to you. The ones who treat it as cheap labour will get nothing out of it. The ones who treat it as a six-month interview will find their next permanent hire without posting a single advert.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Name one team that could absorb a funded six-month placement in the new year, and the person who would actually mentor them. If you can answer both, you are most of the way to a free trial run of your next recruit.

04 · Recruitment · People Management · November 2026

Three in four recruiters now think the robots are writing the CVs.

Here is the plot twist nobody ordered. Having spent last year worrying that employers were using artificial intelligence to sift candidates, we now have the mirror image: around three in four UK recruiters believe the majority of CVs crossing their desk were written by AI. Application volumes have surged, inboxes are heaving, and the humble covering letter has quietly become a Turing test that everybody is failing.

The awkward truth is that both sides are now using machines to talk past each other. Candidates generate polished, keyword-perfect applications in seconds; employers run them through a filter that strips the polish straight back off. The signal that used to tell you someone could write, think and bother has gone flat. Which means the decisions that matter are quietly moving back to the one place AI cannot fake convincingly yet: a proper conversation with a human being.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Stop scoring the covering letter and put that weight into a short, structured first conversation instead. If the CV is now written by a machine, your shortlist should be earned in the room, not in the inbox.

05 · Employee value proposition · GRiD / Employee Benefits · November 2026

You picked the benefits for their added value. Your people never noticed.

Group risk body GRiD reports that 68% of employers chose at least one employee benefit specifically for the extra services bolted onto it, the counselling line, the second-opinion service, the virtual GP, rather than the core cover itself. Employers have clearly got the memo that a benefit is more than the payout. The problem lands in the next finding: the same research keeps showing that employers badly overestimate how much staff actually notice, understand or value what they are given.

So there is a gap, and it is not a money gap. You are buying thoughtful, well-chosen extras and then filing the paperwork where nobody will ever read it. An EVP that exists only in the broker's brochure is not an EVP, it is a cost. The fix is almost embarrassingly cheap: the benefits you already pay for are worth far more the moment someone remembers they exist.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Before you add anything new, run one plain-English reminder of the three best things you already offer and how to use them this week. You will get more value from that than from the next shiny perk.

Grow your own: the welders will not grow themselves.

06 · Wellbeing · GRiD / Health & Protection · November 2026

The review you ignored in spring now sits in most wellbeing plans.

The Keep Britain Working review, Sir Charlie Mayfield's government-commissioned look at how to stop ill health pushing people out of work, has gone from obscure to unavoidable in a matter of months. GRiD now finds that around three quarters of employers are aware of it, awareness tends to thin out among smaller businesses, and the latest government update has promised a dedicated unit to monitor workplace health and wellbeing. For a review most HR teams could not have named at Easter, that is quite a glow-up.

The caution is worth stating plainly. Awareness is not the same as action, and employers are openly split on whether the review will deliver real support or just more reporting. But the direction of travel is now clear enough to plan around: keeping people well enough to stay in work is moving from a nice-to-have to something you may one day be asked to show your workings on. Better to have those workings.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Ask one question at your next review: when someone develops a health problem, do we have a route that keeps them in work, or only a route that manages them out? If it is the latter, that is the gap the next few years will expose.

07 · Working parents · GOV.UK / Pinsent Masons · Autumn 2026

A right that did not exist a year ago: leave to grieve a pregnancy loss.

The government has confirmed the detail of a new day-one right to bereavement leave, and crucially it will extend to parents who suffer a pregnancy loss before 24 weeks. Until now, a miscarriage before that threshold came with no statutory leave at all. Under the Employment Rights Act, eligible parents will be entitled to at least a week away, from their first day in the job, with no qualifying service to earn first. It is one of the quieter clauses in the Act, and one of the most human.

For working parents this closes a gap that has caused real, avoidable hurt, people taking a sick day to grieve a loss the law did not recognise. For employers it is mostly about readiness rather than cost: a manager who fumbles this moment will be remembered for years, and a manager who handles it kindly will be too. The leave is the floor. The way it is handled is the thing people actually carry with them.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Write the bereavement and pregnancy-loss policy before you need it, and make sure line managers know it is a day-one right. The worst time to work out what to say is the morning someone tells you.

08 · Employing people with seen and unseen disabilities · TUC · November 2026

From this month's pay-gap day, disabled workers are effectively working for free.

The TUC has marked its annual Disability Pay Gap Day, the point in the year at which, on average, disabled workers stop being paid relative to their non-disabled colleagues. The union's analysis puts the gap at around 15.5%, worth roughly 2.24 pounds an hour, which is the basis for the "working for free until New Year" framing. The gap has narrowed a little, but the TUC's point stands: it is still large, still stubborn, and still largely invisible because so few employers measure it.

And that is the quietly useful bit for the rest of us. You cannot close a gap you have never counted, and disability pay-gap reporting is not yet mandatory, which means most organisations genuinely do not know their own number. The ones who choose to work it out, voluntarily, are not virtue-signalling. They are simply the only ones in a position to do anything about it before they are told to.

Worth flagging in your next ELT meeting

Calculate your disability pay gap once, even roughly, even just internally. The number itself does the persuading, and you would far rather discover it yourselves than have a future reporting duty discover it for you.
